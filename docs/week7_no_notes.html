Exhibition vs Research Output

Complementary, not opposed

Lecture 7 of 8 — Practice as Research

Mick Grierson, Creative Computing Institute, UAL

Where we are

Weeks 1–3: research, knowledge, the gap

Week 4: methods

Weeks 5–6: reflection and documentation

Today: your work will be shown. It will also be submitted as research. Are these the same thing?

Short answer: no — but they should support each other.

Two different things

Exhibition / demo → creates an experience. Success = how the work is encountered.

Research output → makes an argument. Success = whether the contribution is clear and supported.

Same artefact can sit at the centre of both. The framing, audience, and measure of success differ.

The "show or tell" problem

Rust & Robertson (2003):

An exhibition shows — but often doesn't tell you what the contribution is.

A paper tells — but doesn't let you encounter the work.

Each does something the other can't. Use both deliberately.

The live and the recorded

Peggy Phelan (1993): watching a recording is a different experience from the live event.

For installation, performance, and interactive work this matters: your documentation is not the work. It's a new representation, with its own affordances and limits.

Document thoughtfully — and be honest in your writing about what it captures and what it loses.

What an exhibition does well

Experiential knowledge — scale, presence, duration

Aesthetic character — what no description conveys

Interactivity — only understood by doing

Affective impact — the visceral response

Where the contribution is experiential (Lecture 2's non-propositional knowledge), the exhibition is where part of the knowledge lives.

What a research output does well

The claim — what's new?

The context — the field, the gap (Lecture 3)

The method — how, and why (Lecture 4)

The reasoning — question → evidence → conclusion

Permanence — readable, citable, build-on-able in ten years

Exhibition: ephemeral and experiential. Research output: permanent and argumentative. You need both.

Don't let one eat the other

Exhibition eats research: slick demo, no articulated contribution. The examiner sees something cool but can't assess what was learned.

Research eats exhibition: thorough text, but the experiential dimension is invisible.

Both fail by treating the two as substitutes. They're complementary channels.

They feed each other

Exhibition → research: showing generates data. How do people respond? Where do they get confused? The show can be a method (Lecture 4).

Research → exhibition: knowing your contribution sharpens what you foreground — framing, sequencing, what to show.

The exhibition is a research instrument, not just a destination.

Designing the show for research value

Framing — a short wall text. Orient, don't over-explain.

Capture the encounter — with consent, record how people engage.

Design for documentation — sightlines, lighting, clean capture.

A permanent record — video, code, stills, a project page.

Not a catalogue

Your research output is not a description of what you exhibited.

A catalogue says: here is the work; it looks like this.

A research output says: the field lacks X; I built this to investigate X; here's what it revealed; here's why it matters.

The work and its exhibition are evidence — not the argument itself.

Examined together

Candy (2006), Lecture 2: artefact and writing are examined together. Neither alone suffices.

The writing points to the artefact as evidence.

The artefact embodies what the writing can't fully say.

The documentation bridges the two.

Your final-weeks job: make artefact, documentation, and argument reinforce each other.

Example: Massive Attack vs MAGNet (Barbican, 2019)

The event: a neural audio system (MAGNet) reinterpreting Mezzanine, live.

What it did: let an audience hear neural audio synthesis in 2019 — texture, artefacts, uncanny familiarity. No paper conveys that.

The research output: situated it in the field, explained the method, made the claim.

They fed each other: the event tested whether outputs were compelling at scale; the framing clarified what was being demonstrated.

Same logic, student scale

No matter where you are exhibiting, the logic still holds.

A movement-driven generative visual system at the degree show:

Exhibition: visitors move, the system responds, some try to break it, some don't realise they're the input.

Research output: states the gap, the method, what it reveals about embodied interaction.

They feed each other: who "got it" becomes evidence; the framing shapes the wall text.

Common mistakes

"The degree show is the deliverable." It's one channel; the research output is another. Assessed together.

"My video is my installation." No (Phelan). Different artefact — say what it loses.

"I'll write it up after the show." The observational evidence is gone unless you capture it. Plan capture first.

"The work speaks for itself." With no framing it speaks an experience, not a claim.

Exercise: two framings of one work

Pick a project — yours, or one you admire. Write:

Exhibition framing (~40 words) — wall text for someone encountering it cold. What's the way in?

Research framing (~40 words) — the opening of a research account. State the gap and the claim.

Same work, two voices. 10 minutes — we'll compare a couple.
