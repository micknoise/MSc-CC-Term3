Today: **How do you capture what you learn through making — and turn it into research evidence?**

Two connected problems:

1. **Reflection** — how do you notice and analyse what's happening in your own practice?
2. **Documentation** — how do you record it so that it becomes communicable to others?

> **Speaker note:** These are not afterthoughts. They are core research activities. If you don't reflect, you don't learn from your own process. If you don't document, nobody else can learn from it either. The strongest practice-based submissions are the ones where the maker clearly paid attention to what was happening while they were working, and captured it in a form that others can engage with.

## Slide 3 — Why reflection matters

In Lecture 2 we said practice produces **tacit** and **experiential** knowledge — knowledge that resists full verbalisation.

The problem: if it resists verbalisation, how do you include it in a research submission?

The answer is not "make it fully propositional." Some knowledge will remain partially tacit — and that's fine. The answer is: **reflect on your practice carefully enough that you can articulate as much as possible, and point to the artefact for the rest.**

Reflection is the bridge between doing and knowing. It is what turns "I made a thing" into "here is what making the thing revealed."

> **Speaker note:** Connect back to Borgdorff (Lecture 2): the artefact carries epistemic content that isn't fully reducible to propositions. Your job is not to drain the artefact of all its knowledge and pour it into text. Your job is to articulate what you can, and to present the artefact alongside the text so that the reader/examiner can encounter both. Reflection is how you figure out what you *can* articulate.

## Slide 4 — Schön: the reflective practitioner

Donald Schön (1983) identified two modes of reflection that professionals use:

**Reflection-in-action** — thinking *while* you're doing. The moment you hear your generative system produce an unexpected timbre and you adjust the parameters in response — that's reflection-in-action. You're reading the situation and responding to it in real time.

**Reflection-on-action** — thinking *after* you've done something. You step back from the session, look at what happened, and ask: what worked, what didn't, what surprised me, what does this mean for the next iteration?

Both are research activities, but only the second one reliably produces *records*. The challenge is capturing reflection-in-action before it fades — because in-the-moment insights are often the most valuable and the most perishable.

> **Speaker note:** Schön's framework comes from studying architects, engineers, and other professionals — people who think through making. It maps directly onto what creative computing students do. The problem is that reflection-in-action is often invisible even to the person doing it. You adjust a parameter because it "feels right" — but there's knowledge in that judgement. Reflection-on-action is where you try to unpack what "feels right" actually meant. Both matter, but you need habits and tools to capture them.

## Slide 5 — Reflection is not description

There is a crucial difference between describing what you did and reflecting on it.

**Description:** "I trained the model for 200 epochs on the vocal dataset."

**Reflection:** "I trained the model for 200 epochs on the vocal dataset. At epoch 80, the outputs started producing recognisable vowel shapes but with metallic artefacts. I initially treated this as a training failure, but on listening more carefully I realised the artefacts had a timbral character that was musically interesting — closer to a processed vocal than a clean one. This changed my framing: instead of aiming for high-fidelity vocal reproduction, I began exploring what the model's 'errors' offered as a distinct sonic palette. The gap in my project shifted from 'can neural networks reproduce voice?' to 'what happens when they fail to, and is that failure aesthetically productive?'"

The first is a log entry. The second is a research insight.

> **Speaker note:** Read both versions aloud. The difference is stark. The first tells you what happened; the second tells you what it *meant*. The reflection identifies a surprise, interprets it, and connects it to the research question. That's the move students need to learn. Not every log entry will contain a revelation like this — but if you're not writing entries detailed enough to *allow* for one, you'll miss the revelations when they come.

## Slide 6 — A framework for reflective notes

When you sit down to reflect on a session of making, ask yourself four questions:

**What happened?** — Describe the activity. What did you do, build, test, change?

**What surprised you?** — Surprises are where learning lives. Expected outcomes confirm what you already knew. Unexpected outcomes teach you something new.

**What does it mean?** — Interpret the surprise. Why did it happen? What does it reveal about your system, your assumptions, your question?

**What changes?** — How does this affect what you do next? Does it refine your question? Alter your method? Open a new direction?

This cycle — do, notice, interpret, adjust — is the iterative cyclic web from Lecture 2, operating at the scale of a single work session.

> **Speaker note:** These four questions are a simplified version of Kolb's experiential learning cycle filtered through Schön. Students don't need to cite Kolb — they need to ask the questions. Suggest they keep these four questions taped to their monitor or written at the top of their log. Not every session will produce meaningful answers to all four, but the habit of asking is what matters.

## Slide 7 — Why documentation matters

Documentation serves three audiences:

**Your future self** — in three months you will not remember why you made that design decision, what that parameter value was, or what the output sounded like before you changed the architecture. Your log is your memory.

**Your examiner** — they weren't in the room. They didn't hear the output, see the failure, or feel the surprise. Your documentation is their only access to the parts of your research that the final artefact alone doesn't convey.

**The field** — if your work is good, other people will want to build on it. They need to know what you did, how you did it, and what you learned. Reproducibility and extendability depend on documentation.

> **Speaker note:** The "future self" argument is the one that lands most immediately with students. Anyone who's ever returned to an old project and found they can't remember why the code is structured the way it is knows this pain. Research documentation is just good engineering practice taken seriously. The examiner argument is the one that matters most for assessment. The field argument is the one that matters most for contribution.

## Slide 8 — What to document: the five layers

Think of documentation as operating at five layers, from the most granular to the most interpretive:

**1. The artefact itself** — the system, the code, the outputs, the installation, the performance. This is the primary evidence.

**2. Technical specification** — architecture, dependencies, parameters, training data, hardware. Enough detail that someone could, in principle, reproduce or extend your work.

**3. Process record** — what you did, in what order, with what results. Dated, sequential, factual. This is your lab notebook.

**4. Reflective commentary** — the four-question framework from slide 6. What surprised you, what it meant, what changed. This is where tacit knowledge becomes partially explicit.

**5. Critical framing** — how the artefact and the process relate to the research question, the gap, and the field. This is the interpretive layer that turns documentation into argument.

Layers 1–3 are **records**. Layer 4 is **reflection**. Layer 5 is **analysis**. You need all five, but they serve different purposes and often live in different places.

> **Speaker note:** Walk through each layer with a concrete example. Layer 1: "here's the system running." Layer 2: "here's the README with architecture details and how to run the code." Layer 3: "here's my process log showing that I tried three different loss functions before settling on this one." Layer 4: "here's my reflective note explaining that the spectral loss produced more musically interesting results than the waveform loss, which surprised me because the literature favours waveform loss for perceptual quality." Layer 5: "here's my argument that this reveals a tension between perceptual fidelity and aesthetic interest in neural audio synthesis." Each layer builds on the ones below it.

## Slide 9 — Where documentation lives

Different layers live in different places. Here's a practical breakdown:

**Code and system:** GitHub or GitLab. Use meaningful commit messages — they are process documentation for free. Tag significant versions. Write a proper README.

**Process log:** A regular document — Markdown, Notion, a physical notebook, whatever you'll actually use. Date every entry. Aim for at least one entry per working session.

**Audio/visual outputs:** Timestamped exports. Keep a folder structure that makes sense. Name files meaningfully — `output_2025-03-14_spectral-loss-v2.wav` not `test7_final_FINAL.wav`.

**Reflective notes:** In the process log, or as a separate layer on top of it. Some people prefer a separate reflective journal; others annotate their process log.

**Video and screen recordings:** For installations, performances, interactive systems, and anything where the experience is temporal. OBS is free. Record more than you think you need.

**The written submission:** Where layers 4 and 5 ultimately live in their polished form.

> **Speaker note:** The practical detail matters here. Students know they "should" document, but they don't know what that looks like day to day. Be specific: "after every significant working session, spend 10 minutes writing in your log." On commit messages: a message that says "fixed stuff" is useless as process documentation. A message that says "replaced MSE loss with multi-resolution STFT loss — MSE was producing smooth but lifeless outputs" is a reflective note embedded in your version history.

## Slide 10 — The annotated portfolio

Gaver & Bowers (2012) proposed the **annotated portfolio** as a method for presenting design artefacts as research contributions.

The idea: you present a curated selection of artefacts (prototypes, outputs, iterations, experiments) and accompany each with written annotation that explains **what it shows, why it matters, and how it relates to the others**.

The portfolio is not a gallery. It is an **argument made through artefacts**.

Each annotation should address:

- What is this artefact? (What was it made with, when, in what context?)
- What does it demonstrate? (What knowledge does it carry?)
- What design decisions does it embody? (What choices were made, and why?)
- How does it relate to the other items in the portfolio? (What trajectory or development does the collection reveal?)

> **Speaker note:** The annotated portfolio is especially powerful for creative computing because so much of the work is iterative — students produce many versions, prototypes, and experiments on the way to a final system. Without annotation, these are just a pile of files. With annotation, they become evidence of a research process. Gaver & Bowers originally published this in Interactions (ACM) and it's been widely adopted in design research and HCI. It's a legitimate, citable method.

## Slide 11 — An annotated portfolio in practice

Imagine you're building a system that generates visual patterns from audio input.

**Item 1:** Early prototype. Direct amplitude-to-brightness mapping.
*Annotation:* "The first mapping was literal — loud = bright, quiet = dark. The result was visually responsive but uninteresting. It confirmed that direct parameter mapping produces correlation without aesthetic surprise."

**Item 2:** Second prototype. Spectral centroid mapped to colour hue, onset density mapped to pattern complexity.
*Annotation:* "By mapping perceptual audio features to visual properties, the system began producing outputs that 'felt' connected to the sound rather than merely synchronised with it. The distinction between synchronisation and connection became a central concept in the project."

**Item 3:** Final system. Multi-feature mapping with a learned crossmodal embedding.
*Annotation:* "The learned embedding produced unexpected cross-modal associations — certain timbres consistently evoked angular shapes, which aligns with research on sound symbolism (Köhler, 1929; Ramachandran & Hubbard, 2001). The system's outputs became evidence for a relationship between audio and visual features that was discovered through making, not predicted by theory."

Three items. Three annotations. A clear trajectory from naive mapping to discovered insight.

> **Speaker note:** Walk through this slowly. The trajectory is the key — it's not three disconnected experiments, it's a directed path where each iteration was informed by what the previous one revealed. That's the iterative cyclic web made visible through documentation. The annotations are doing the reflective work: they don't just say what was built, they say what was *learned*. The final annotation connects the making back to existing literature — that's layer 5 (critical framing) entering the documentation.

## Slide 12 — Documenting failure

Failures, dead ends, and abandoned directions are **research evidence**, not embarrassments.

If you tried an approach and it didn't work, that's valuable information — provided you document *why* it didn't work and *what you learned from it*.

A project that presents only its final, successful outcome looks less rigorous than one that shows: "I tried A, B, and C. A failed because of X. B was partially successful but had limitation Y. C, informed by what I learned from A and B, addressed both X and Y."

The narrative of informed iteration is one of the strongest things you can present in a practice-based submission.

> **Speaker note:** Students are often reluctant to include failures because they think it makes them look incompetent. The opposite is true. A project with no documented failures looks like the student got lucky on the first try — or, more likely, that they didn't try anything ambitious enough to fail. Failure is where the interesting learning happens. Document it. Explain it. Show how it shaped what came next.

## Slide 13 — Documenting for different audiences

Not all documentation goes in the final submission. Think of it as concentric rings:

**Inner ring — for you:** The raw process log. Everything. Messy, dated, honest. This is your working memory. It stays in your notebook or your private repo.

**Middle ring — for the examiner:** The curated evidence. Selected log entries, annotated portfolio items, key outputs, the reflective commentary that supports your argument. This goes in the appendices or the body of your submission.

**Outer ring — for the field:** The reproducibility package. Code on GitHub with a README, trained models if shareable, a clear description of the method. This is what lets others build on your work.

You write the inner ring first and extract the other two from it. If the inner ring doesn't exist, there's nothing to extract.

> **Speaker note:** The concentric rings model is practical. Students don't need to write beautifully in their process log — they need to write *enough* that they can later extract the good bits. The middle ring is where the curation happens: you select the moments, artefacts, and reflections that support your research argument. The outer ring is about generosity to the field — if your code is on GitHub with a decent README, you've made a contribution even if nobody reads your thesis.

## Slide 14 — What "good enough" documentation looks like

You don't need a cinematic production diary. You need:

**Process log:** One entry per significant working session. 100–300 words. Uses the four-question framework (what happened, what surprised you, what does it mean, what changes). Dated.

**Version-controlled code:** With meaningful commit messages. Tagged at significant milestones. A README that explains how to run it, what it does, and what it depends on.

**Timestamped outputs:** Audio, images, video — whatever your system produces. Named sensibly. Kept in chronological order so you can trace the development.

**At least one screen recording or demo video** of your system in action. Even if it's rough. The examiner needs to see the thing working, not just read about it.

**Reflective notes** at key decision points: when you changed direction, when something failed, when something surprised you, when you made a significant design choice.

This is not a huge overhead. It's 15 minutes of writing per session, plus basic file hygiene.

> **Speaker note:** Emphasise the "15 minutes per session" point. Students imagine documentation as a separate, enormous task. It isn't — it's a small habit embedded in the working process. The alternative is trying to reconstruct six months of work from memory in the final two weeks. Nobody writes a good reflective account retrospectively. You write it as you go, and then you edit it into shape.

## Slide 15 — Documentation and the iterative cyclic web

Smith & Dean's iterative cyclic web (Lecture 2) describes a process of making, reflecting, reading, and revising — repeatedly.

Documentation is how you **make that cycle visible**. Without it, the cycle happens but nobody (including you) can trace it afterwards.

Each pass through the cycle should leave a trace:

- **Make** → outputs, code commits, prototypes.
- **Reflect** → log entries, reflective notes.
- **Read** → annotated references, notes on how the reading changed your thinking.
- **Revise** → updated question, changed parameters, new direction — documented with a note explaining *why* you revised.

If your documentation lets someone reconstruct the trajectory of your thinking — from first hunch to final artefact, through all the surprises and pivots — you have a strong evidential foundation for your submission.

> **Speaker note:** This is the conceptual integration point of the lecture: documentation operationalises the iterative cyclic web. It's not a separate activity bolted onto research — it *is* how research gets recorded. The trace of the cycle is the evidence base from which the final written submission is constructed. Without it, the writing becomes speculation about what you think happened, rather than analysis of what actually did.

## Slide 16 — A worked example: documenting a project in creative computing

Here's what good documentation might look like across the life of a 12-week MSc project:

**Weeks 1–3:** Process log established. Research question drafted. Literature notes with five-point extraction (from Lecture 3). Gap statement drafted. Early sketches and architectural diagrams.

**Weeks 4–6:** First prototype built. Code on GitHub. Initial outputs exported and timestamped. Log entries note first surprises and pivots. Reflective notes at key decision points ("chose spectral loss over waveform loss because…").

**Weeks 7–9:** Second iteration built, informed by reflections on the first. User study conducted (if applicable). Screen recordings and demo videos captured. Annotated portfolio beginning to take shape — three or four items with annotations.

**Weeks 10–12:** Final system documented. Process log reviewed and key entries extracted for the written submission. Annotated portfolio finalised. Reflective commentary written up as part of the methods and discussion sections. README polished. Code tagged as final version.

> **Speaker note:** This timeline is realistic for a taught MSc. The important thing is that documentation is distributed across the whole project, not crammed into the end. Students who log as they go will find the final writing much easier — they're editing and curating existing material, not generating it from scratch. Students who don't log will spend their last two weeks trying to remember what they did in week 4. Don't be that person.

## Slide 17 — Common mistakes

**"I'll document later."** You won't. Or you will, but you'll forget the insights that mattered most. Document as you go.

**"My code is my documentation."** Code is layer 2 (technical specification). It doesn't capture why you made the choices you did, what surprised you, or how your understanding changed. You need layers 3, 4, and 5 as well.

**"I documented everything."** If you documented *everything* equally, you documented nothing usefully. Documentation for the examiner is curated — you select the moments that matter and explain why they matter.

**"The artefact speaks for itself."** It doesn't — not to someone who wasn't in the room while you made it. The artefact speaks *with* the documentation. Alone, it's mute.

**"Reflection means saying what I felt."** Reflection is analytical, not emotional. "I felt frustrated" is not a research insight. "The system consistently failed at long sustained notes, which frustrated me, but which also revealed that the architecture handles transients better than steady-state signals — a finding I hadn't anticipated" — that's a research insight.

> **Speaker note:** The last point is worth emphasising. Students sometimes confuse reflection with introspection. Emotional responses can be starting points — frustration, delight, surprise are all signals that something worth examining has happened. But the reflection is the examination, not the emotion. "I was surprised" opens a door; the reflection walks through it and reports what's on the other side.

## Slide 18 — An exercise: write a reflective note

Think about the last significant thing you worked on — for this module, for another project, or in your own practice.

Write a short reflective note (150–200 words) using the four-question framework:

1. **What happened?**
2. **What surprised you?**
3. **What does it mean?**
4. **What changes?**

Take 10 minutes. We'll hear a couple of examples.

> **Speaker note:** This is the in-class exercise. Keep it to 10 minutes of writing, then 5 minutes hearing 2–3 examples. The point is to experience how quickly reflection becomes productive once you have a structure. Students who say "nothing surprised me" are either not looking carefully enough or working on something too safe. Push gently: "really? Nothing went differently from what you expected?" Surprise is everywhere if you're paying attention.
