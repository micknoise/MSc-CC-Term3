## Slide 1 — Title

**Exhibition vs Research Output**
Complementary, not opposed

Lecture 7 of 8 — Practice as Research
Mick Grierson, [Creative Computing Institute](https://www.arts.ac.uk/creative-computing-institute), UAL

> **Speaker note:** We're in the final stretch — the "making it land" phase. Students now have a question, a gap, a method, and (hopefully) a habit of documentation. This week addresses a confusion that trips up almost everyone in creative computing: the difference between showing your work and explaining your research. They're related but not the same, and conflating them weakens both.

---

## Slide 2 — Where we are

- Weeks 1–3: research, knowledge, the gap
- Week 4: methods
- Weeks 5–6: reflection and documentation

**Today:** your work will be *shown*. It will also be *submitted* as research. Are these the same thing?

Short answer: **no — but they should support each other.**

> **Speaker note:** Most students assume that if they make an impressive demo and exhibit it well, the research takes care of itself. It doesn't. An exhibition and a research output answer different questions for different audiences. Today is about understanding the difference and designing for both without letting one cannibalise the other.

---

## Slide 3 — Two different things

**Exhibition / demo** → creates an *experience*. Success = how the work is encountered.

**Research output** → makes an *argument*. Success = whether the contribution is clear and supported.

Same artefact can sit at the centre of both. The framing, audience, and measure of success differ.

> **Speaker note:** The same generative installation can be (a) a compelling thing to stand in front of at a degree show, and (b) the centrepiece of a research argument about, say, audience perception of machine creativity. Not in conflict — but each needs different supporting material. The exhibition needs good lighting, a sightline, a short wall text. The research output needs a gap statement, a method, documentation, and an argument. Same artefact, two presentations.

---

## Slide 4 — The "show or tell" problem

Rust & Robertson (2003):

- An exhibition **shows** — but often doesn't *tell* you what the contribution is.
- A paper **tells** — but doesn't let you *encounter* the work.

Each does something the other can't. Use both deliberately.

> **Speaker note:** Rust and Robertson organised a refereed research exhibition at a Design Research Society conference and reflected on what worked. Their key finding: exhibitions need a "full narrative within the exhibit" and a "permanent record" — otherwise the research content evaporates the moment the show closes. That's the central practical lesson: showing and telling are complementary, and a strong submission does both.

---

## Slide 5 — The live and the recorded

Peggy Phelan (1993): watching a recording is a *different* experience from the live event.

For installation, performance, and interactive work this matters: **your documentation is not the work.** It's a new representation, with its own affordances and limits.

Document thoughtfully — and be honest in your writing about what it captures and what it loses.

> **Speaker note:** Phelan's argument is contested and she's writing about live performance specifically, but the underlying point is vital for this cohort. Students often think a video of their installation IS their installation. It isn't — it can't reproduce scale, presence, interactivity, or the embodied experience of being in the space. Not a reason to give up on documentation, but a reason to document carefully and to be explicit about its limits.

## Slide 6 — What an exhibition does well

- **Experiential knowledge** — scale, presence, duration
- **Aesthetic character** — what no description conveys
- **Interactivity** — only understood by doing
- **Affective impact** — the visceral response

Where the contribution is experiential (Lecture 2's non-propositional knowledge), the exhibition is *where part of the knowledge lives*.


> **Speaker note:** This connects to Borgdorff and non-conceptual knowledge from Lecture 2. If your contribution includes "this system produces a particular kind of aesthetic experience," the only way to fully convey it is to let people experience it. The exhibition isn't decoration — it's a venue where part of the research is made available. The catch: exhibition alone won't articulate the *claim*. That's the next slide. The image — an immersive installation where visitors become part of the work — is exactly the kind of piece that text can't reproduce.

---

## Slide 7 — What a research output does well

- **The claim** — what's new?
- **The context** — the field, the gap (Lecture 3)
- **The method** — how, and why (Lecture 4)
- **The reasoning** — question → evidence → conclusion
- **Permanence** — readable, citable, build-on-able in ten years

Exhibition: ephemeral and experiential. Research output: permanent and argumentative. You need both.

> **Speaker note:** The symmetry is the point: slide 6 listed what exhibitions do that text can't; this lists what text does that exhibitions can't. They're complementary, not ranked. A degree show with no research output makes no explicit knowledge claim. A research output with no work (or documentation) is an argument with no evidence.

---

## Slide 8 — Don't let one eat the other

**Exhibition eats research:** slick demo, no articulated contribution. The examiner sees something cool but can't assess what was *learned*.

**Research eats exhibition:** thorough text, but the experiential dimension is invisible.

Both fail by treating the two as substitutes. They're complementary channels.

> **Speaker note:** Both failure modes are common at degree-show time. Art-leaning students tend toward the first; engineering-leaning students toward the second. Name it gently. The fix is the same for both: decide early which parts of your contribution are experiential (→ exhibition) and which are argumentative (→ written output), and resource both deliberately.

---

## Slide 9 — They feed each other

**Exhibition → research:** showing generates data. How do people respond? Where do they get confused? The show can be a *method* (Lecture 4).

**Research → exhibition:** knowing your contribution sharpens what you foreground — framing, sequencing, what to show.

The exhibition is a research *instrument*, not just a destination.

> **Speaker note:** This is the constructive heart of the lecture. Watching people interact with your installation at the degree show is exactly the observation we discussed in Lecture 4. Capture it: take notes, record with consent (Lecture 4 ethics), note the questions people ask. That becomes material for your written discussion.

---

## Slide 10 — Designing the show for research value

- **Framing** — a short wall text. Orient, don't over-explain.
- **Capture the encounter** — with consent, record how people engage.
- **Design for documentation** — sightlines, lighting, clean capture.
- **A permanent record** — video, code, stills, a project page.

*Gallery interior with framing text alongside the work. Source: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA.*

> **Speaker note:** Concrete, do-able things. The wall-text balance is subtle — too little and viewers don't grasp the research; too much and you flatten the experience into an illustrated lecture. Aim for a sentence or two that gives a way in. "Design for documentation" connects back to Weeks 5–6: if students have documented all along, the exhibition record is just the final, most public layer.

## Slide 11 — Not a catalogue

Your research output is **not** a description of what you exhibited.

- A catalogue says: *here is the work; it looks like this.*
- A research output says: *the field lacks X; I built this to investigate X; here's what it revealed; here's why it matters.*

The work and its exhibition are **evidence** — not the argument itself.

> **Speaker note:** The inverse of slide 4's "show or tell." Just as an exhibition without framing fails to tell, a written output that only describes fails to argue. Students sometimes write the dissertation as an extended artist's statement — lyrical, descriptive, no falsifiable claim, no literature. Push them back to the structure from Weeks 1–4: question, gap, method, evidence, contribution.

---

## Slide 12 — Examined together

Candy (2006), Lecture 2: artefact and writing are **examined together**. Neither alone suffices.

- The writing **points to** the artefact as evidence.
- The artefact **embodies** what the writing can't fully say.
- The documentation **bridges** the two.

Your final-weeks job: make artefact, documentation, and argument reinforce each other.

> **Speaker note:** This brings Candy's model (Lecture 2, slide 13) full circle. The MSc isn't a PhD, but the logic holds: artefact + writing, examined together, with documentation as connective tissue. If a student kept the five-layer documentation from Weeks 5–6, this binding is already half-done — the reflective and critical layers are exactly what connect the shown artefact to the written claim.

## Slide 13 — Example: Massive Attack vs MAGNet (Barbican, 2019)

- **The event:** a neural audio system (MAGNet) reinterpreting *Mezzanine*, live.
- **What it did:** let an audience *hear* neural audio synthesis in 2019 — texture, artefacts, uncanny familiarity. No paper conveys that.
- **The research output:** situated it in the field, explained the method, made the claim.
- **They fed each other:** the event tested whether outputs were compelling at scale; the framing clarified what was being demonstrated.


> **Speaker note:** Make the abstraction concrete. The Barbican event and the research account of it are two channels for one contribution. The event carried the experiential knowledge — what it's *like* to hear an album reimagined by a neural network. The research carried the claim, context, and method. Neither alone is sufficient: a paper without the performance loses the experience; the performance without the paper makes no citable contribution. (Image is illustrative of scale and live audience, not the event itself — swap in your own Barbican documentation if you have it.)

## Slide 14 — Same logic, student scale

No matter where you are exhibiting, the logic still holds.

A movement-driven generative visual system at the degree show:

- **Exhibition:** visitors move, the system responds, some try to break it, some don't realise they're the input.
- **Research output:** states the gap, the method, what it reveals about embodied interaction.
- **They feed each other:** who "got it" becomes evidence; the framing shapes the wall text.

> **Speaker note:** The realistic version for the cohort — Barbican logic and degree-show logic are identical, only the scale differs. Encourage students to treat the degree show as a research event, not just a display. The people walking through are, in effect, study participants (mind the Lecture 4 ethics). What they do is data.

## Slide 15 — Common mistakes

- **"The degree show is the deliverable."** It's one channel; the research output is another. Assessed together.
- **"My video is my installation."** No (Phelan). Different artefact — say what it loses.
- **"I'll write it up after the show."** The observational evidence is gone unless you capture it. Plan capture first.
- **"The work speaks for itself."** With no framing it speaks an *experience*, not a *claim*.

> **Speaker note:** The third point costs students the most. Observational data from a live show is perishable — capture it in the moment or lose it. Tie back to Weeks 5–6: documentation extends to the exhibition itself. The show is the last and most public entry in the process log.

## Slide 16 — Exercise: two framings of one work

Pick a project — yours, or one you admire. Write:

1. **Exhibition framing (~40 words)** — wall text for someone encountering it cold. What's the way in?
2. **Research framing (~40 words)** — the opening of a research account. State the gap and the claim.

Same work, two voices. 10 minutes — we'll compare a couple.

> **Speaker note:** 10 minutes writing, ~5 sharing. The learning is in the contrast: the exhibition framing is invitational and experiential; the research framing is argumentative and situated. If the two collapse into one text, the student hasn't yet separated experience from claim. Push: "the wall text makes me walk over. The research framing tells me why the field should care. Different sentences."
